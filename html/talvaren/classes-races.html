Adventurer Classes (Direct Confrontation)

  • Warrior

    Weapon masters who refuse to lose once battle begins.

    Category
    Adventurer
    Combat tendency
    Not yet established
    Signature utility
    Not yet established
    Paths
    Not yet established

    Warriors are masters of their chosen craft. They study weapons, armor, movement, timing, and the thousand practical decisions that determine how a fight ends.

    They are not limited to the role of a traditional brute or shield-bearing tank. A Warrior may fight for honor, power, loyalty, survival, or simply because they enjoy the violence. Whatever drives them, they become exceptionally difficult to stop once they commit to a battle.

    Warriors understand how to end fights. They know when to press, when to endure, when to exploit weakness, and when fairness is nothing more than a needless handicap. They can stand beside allies, challenge an opponent directly, or use every advantage available to secure victory.

    A Warrior is among the toughest fighters in Talvaren—not because they are mindless brutes, but because they understand conflict, master their chosen craft, and refuse to lose once the fight has begun.

    -The Archivist

    Rogues are the eyes of every group. Their training teaches them to notice what others miss, recognize danger before it announces itself, and understand how threats are built.

    They specialize in traps, detection, removal, and practical control of dangerous situations. A Rogue can find mechanisms hidden beneath ordinary surroundings, dismantle what can be safely removed, and guide companions through what cannot. When violence begins, they control the field by disrupting enemies, creating openings, and deciding where attention belongs.

    A Rogue gathers information because knowledge keeps people alive. They scout ahead, identify weaknesses, bypass obstacles, and keep an adventuring party from stumbling blindly into problems someone could have prevented. The quiet approach is useful, but it is not their only answer.

    A Rogue is not merely a sneaky opportunist. They are the expedition’s advance warning, drawing strength from awareness, timing, precision, and the willingness to see danger first—and deal with it before everyone else pays the price.

    -The Archivist

    Priests are guardians, healers, and unyielding defenders of the people placed in their care. Their training teaches them to recognize suffering, understand what caused it, and stand close enough to answer it.

    A Priest does not heal from a safe distance. They witness the wound, face the one responsible, and repay the harm before calling the injured back to strength. Their compassion is not weakness; it is the reason they refuse to let violence go unanswered.

    They serve their communities through ritual, counsel, protection, and action. If danger comes for their home or companions, a Priest takes up whatever weapon and armor the moment requires and advances toward it.

    A Priest is neither a passive caretaker nor a distant miracle worker. They stand at the front, drawing strength from conviction, courage, healing, and the absolute refusal to abandon those under their protection.

    Wizards are masters of their chosen arcane craft. They study systems, patterns, and consequences until magic becomes less a mystery and more a discipline of dangerous, precise, exhausting work.

    They see what the rest of the world does not. A Wizard can recognize arcane deviations hidden within ordinary surroundings—subtle distortions, unnatural patterns, and magical inconsistencies that reveal danger before it announces itself.

    Their strength lies in preparation. Wizards survive because they study, plan, and understand what they are facing before the first spell is cast. They do not rely on improvisation when knowledge, foresight, and careful preparation can turn uncertainty into an advantage.

    In Talvaren, a Wizard is not merely a spellcaster. They are an investigator of the unseen, a master of their chosen craft, and one of the few people capable of noticing when reality itself has been tampered with.

    Necromancers are doctors of broken finality. They study the boundary between life and death, learning how to repair what has been damaged, stabilize what should not yet end, and preserve what others would abandon.

    Their knowledge also teaches them how to unmake those repairs. A Necromancer can dismantle altered states, sever unnatural bindings, and return the dead to stillness when restoration is no longer possible. This makes them especially capable when facing undead or other beings sustained by disrupted death.

    Necromancers are respected throughout Talvaren. Their work is difficult, necessary, and often compassionate, even when outsiders find it unsettling. Some travel with a quiet presence at their side—an old patient, a familiar spirit, or something that has not yet decided whether it is alive.

    A Necromancer is not a master of death. They are a physician who understands what death means, what can be saved, and what must finally be allowed to end.

    -The Archivist

    Outfitters understand material truth, fit, flexibility, and protection. They know how equipment fails—and how to prevent it.

    They work with metal, cloth, leather, hide, layered materials, and the structures that hold weapons, armor, and clothing together. Their skill keeps gear functional through long journeys, harsh weather, hard use, and sudden danger.

    An Outfitter can maintain a team’s equipment, repair what others would discard, and prepare weapons for the road ahead. In combat, they recognize weaknesses in an opponent’s gear and know how to exploit them, turning careful observation into an opening.

    If danger finds their home or traveling companions, Outfitters do not remain behind the workbench. They take up the equipment they prepared, trust what their hands have built, and stand ready to defend what is theirs.

    They are adventurers because equipment does not care where it breaks—and neither does an Outfitter when it is time to strike back.

    -The Archivist

    Jewelers are observant, patient, and steady-handed. Their training teaches them to understand materials, use their tools properly, and recognize the difference between something that is sound and something that is about to fail.

    Those skills remain useful far beyond the workshop. A Jeweler can repair, create, assess, improvise, and defend themselves when circumstances demand it. They are comfortable with the tools of their trade, practical problems, unfamiliar environments, and the ordinary complications of life on the road.

    If trouble arrives at their home or reaches the people under their care, a Jeweler does not need to become someone else. They put down their tools, take up what they have prepared, slip on the rings they crafted, and stand beside those willing to fight.

    A Jeweler is neither a dedicated warrior nor a helpless artisan. They meet the road with preparation, precision, sound judgment, and the knowledge to make the most of what is available.

    Provisioners understand what keeps people healthy, what makes them sick, and how preparation can turn simple ingredients into something useful. Their training teaches them to identify safe food, recognize danger in unfamiliar substances, and create meals, mixtures, and remedies that help others endure difficult journeys.

    They know how to strengthen a body, settle an upset stomach, lift a weary spirit, and give an adventuring group the edge it needs. They also know that not every ingredient belongs in a bowl. Some can be weaponized, scattered, or turned against an enemy.

    A Provisioner’s trusty frying pan is always at their side. If trouble reaches their companions, they put their knowledge to work, reach for their pan, and stand ready to defend what is theirs.

    A Provisioner is both caretaker and combatant. Their strength comes from preparation, practical knowledge, and knowing exactly what the situation calls for.

    Scribes preserve knowledge, routes, laws, and truth. Their maps begin with exploration, and every journey adds detail to Talvaren’s understanding of itself. Most people possess only a basic map of the world. Those who want better information know to visit a Scribe—and pay for accuracy.

    A Scribe’s greatest power is the personal writ: a written law carried, studied, and eventually called forth when circumstances demand it. A writ can define what is permitted, restrict what is possible, or force reality to acknowledge a truth the Scribe has recorded.

    In combat, Scribes turn preparation into control. They mark boundaries, expose violations, and bury enemies beneath summoned paperwork until movement becomes impossible. The more carefully a Scribe understands the world, the more authority their words can carry.

    A Scribe is not a helpless pencil pusher. Their strength comes from observation, preparation, exact language, and knowing that somewhere in Talvaren, there is a form for this.

Notable Figures

  • The Fox

    Rumors, Hearsay, and One Misappropriated Silver Piece

    No reliable record contains The Fox’s birth name.

    One account calls him the cast-off child of a Scribe from a city since scraped from every map. Another insists he stepped fully formed from a traveling show tent, already juggling knives and secrets—and dropping neither.

    A third claims he is older than the first road. That witness also described his own grandmother as older than weather and should not be relied upon for dates.

    The Few Things I Can Prove

    The Fox is a traveling merchant of cosmetics and curiosities. This is the only statement in this entry supported by receipts.

    He follows a fixed circuit between the cities, although no record agrees upon when he began following it. He never lies. Every witness who has dealt with him agrees on this point.

    They also agree that it is the problem.

    The Fox answers the question asked. Whether his answer has anything to do with what the questioner wished to know is apparently considered the questioner’s responsibility.

    Descriptions of him are useless. He has been reported as young, old, tall, stooped, handsome, scarred, clean-shaven, and possessed of a magnificent red beard. Four of these descriptions came from the same town during the same afternoon.

    Most witnesses remember his clothing, his merchandise, or the sound of his laughter. Very few remember his face.

    Considering his profession, this may be intentional.

    The Cinders, Probably

    The Fox’s tent is most often found among those of The Cinders, a traveling company of singers, musicians, tumblers, actors, and fools.

    To say that he travels with them may be an assumption. His tent is present when they arrive and absent after they depart. The Cinders insist that he does not lead them.

    No performance begins until he has taken his seat.

    No camp leaves while his tent remains.

    The most persistent story claims that three Cinders stay behind whenever the company moves on. If this were literally true, the company should have run out of performers years ago.

    It has not.

    Unfamiliar performers appear along the road. No one recalls seeing them audition. The company maintains no dependable roll of membership.

    I requested one.

    The document provided listed forty-two performers, three deceased musicians, and myself under percussion. I do not play percussion. The penmanship was otherwise excellent.

    The repeated claim that three Cinders remain behind is too consistent to dismiss and too inconsistent to verify.

    I therefore placed one silver piece inside a sealed Archive envelope and sent The Fox one plainly worded question:

    Which three?

    The Supposed Network

    Bakers. Blacksmiths. Courtesans. Innkeepers. Lamplighters.

    The Fox’s alleged network always seems to consist of people who hear more than others realize and are remembered less often than they deserve.

    None admit to serving him. Several deny knowing him. Four asked whether he had answered my letter, although I had not mentioned sending one.

    It is said that The Fox does not purchase information with coin. Coin leaves records. He trades in favors, and favors have longer memories.

    No ledger of these favors has been found.

    This proves either that no such network exists or that its accounting is considerably better than ours.

    The Dragon

    One frequently repeated tale claims that Vallinoraxistup, the last living dragon, does not hoard gold.

    He hoards what others know.

    When the dragon wishes to learn where a True Unique sleeps, he is said to send a single scale to The Fox’s tent. By morning the scale is gone. Soon afterward, the dragon moves.

    No witness has seen the scale delivered. No courier has been identified. No answer has been recovered.

    The story nevertheless appears in several cities with nearly identical wording. Ordinarily this would increase its credibility.

    In this case, it does not.

    The True Uniques

    The Fox is rumored to possess three True Uniques.

    He is also rumored to possess fifteen.

    These claims are occasionally made by the same people.

    He has been seen openly displaying none. Some witnesses describe a silver ring among his juggling props that continues spinning after the others fall. Others insist it was brass, a wooden hoop, or not present at all.

    I requested an inventory.

    The Fox sent me a price list for rouge.

    The Answer

    My silver returned six weeks later inside an Archive envelope I do not remember issuing. It bore my own seal.

    Written around the coin’s edge were the following words:

    One to hear.

    One to believe.

    One to tell it again.

    These are not names. I have therefore filed the response under Noncompliance.

    The kobold-shaped shade cast across my desk laughed in absolute delight.

    It has declined to explain why.

    —The Archivist, who has entered one silver piece under Necessary Research and will tolerate no audit of the matter.

Current Playable Peoples

  • humans

    Widespread people defined by adaptability and possibility.

    Homeland
    Tharnhald
    Age expectation
    100 years
    Racial bonus
    Take half damage from Frozen Tundra biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Not yet established

    humans are the most widespread people in Talvaren. They live in cities, villages, fortresses, farmlands, ports, wilderness settlements, and places where survival depends on knowing exactly when to leave. They are found in nearly every environment because humans are exceptionally good at adapting to conditions that would defeat less flexible people.

    Humanity has no single tradition, philosophy, or way of life. human cultures change according to their surroundings, their history, and the pressures placed upon them. Some build carefully ordered societies. Others live by trade, conquest, exploration, faith, craft, or simple endurance. humans can preserve ancient customs for generations, abandon them in a single season, or carry pieces of several cultures into something entirely new.

    They are neither naturally superior nor naturally gifted. Their strength lies in their refusal to remain limited by what they were given. humans learn quickly, copy what works, improve what does not, and turn hardship into experience. They form alliances, exploit opportunities, rebuild after disasters, and settle places other peoples consider unwelcoming.

    This adaptability can make humans inventive, resilient, and remarkably difficult to eliminate. It can also make them restless, ambitious, short-sighted, and dangerously willing to reshape the world around them. humans often disagree about what survival requires, but they rarely stop moving for long.

    In Talvaren, humans are not defined by one calling or destiny. They are defined by possibility. A human may become almost anything: farmer, ruler, scholar, soldier, criminal, explorer, artisan, mystic, or something no one has thought to name yet.

    They endure because they adapt.

    They spread because they can.

    And wherever Talvaren becomes difficult, dangerous, or unfamiliar, humans are usually already there—trying to make a life out of it.

    -The Archivist

  • fey

    Immortal beings shaped by obligation and Bearing.

    Homeland
    Eldevaren
    Age expectation
    Immortal
    Racial bonus
    Take half damage from Jungle biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Not yet established

    Fey are immortal beings shaped by obligation.

    Their homeland is Eldevaren, a realm beyond ordinary reach. Fey may live throughout Talvaren, but the only known method of returning to Eldevaren is the carefully maintained toadstool ring in Vinestrum. Other rings are rumored to exist, hidden in forgotten forests and places where ordinary paths have become unreliable.

    Time does not mark fey as it marks mortal peoples. Instead, their lives are written upon them through Bearing.

    Bearing is the outward expression of promises kept, obligations carried, and consequences accumulated across an immortal life. It may change a fey slowly or transform them completely. One may possess flawless, almost impossible beauty. Another may appear trollish, with tusks, thick skin, immense proportions, or features that mortals mistake for monstrous.

    Neither appearance reveals capability.

    Among the fey, beauty is not proof of goodness, and a monstrous Bearing is not evidence of corruption. Great burdens may leave visible marks. Difficult promises honored across centuries may shape the body more severely than cruelty ever could. A beautiful fey may be honorable, treacherous, newly unburdened, or simply difficult to understand.

    Fey are neither altered humans nor monsters by nature. Their appearances are records, though few outsiders can read them correctly.

    Fey always keep their word.

    This certainty makes bargaining with them more dangerous, not less. A careless promise may remain binding long after its mortal maker has died. An agreement may pass through families, survive kingdoms, and reach descendants who never knew it had been made.

    Fey consider both the words of an agreement and the obligation created by them. They do not treat promises as temporary conveniences. Once terms have been accepted, fulfillment is expected regardless of how circumstances change or how inconvenient the bargain becomes.

    For this reason, bargains with the fey should be struck rarely and thrice checked by a scribe.

    Names, meanings, witnesses, conditions, exceptions, and consequences must be recorded with care. The fey do not need to lie when mortals are perfectly capable of misunderstanding the truth. A bargain may be honored completely and still cost far more than either party expected.

    This has not prevented mortals from bargaining with them.

    Formal agreements between fey and mortal societies have existed for generations. Witnesses and written terms have replaced some of the fear that once surrounded such dealings, but they have not made the bargains harmless. They have only made the danger easier to name, record, and remember.

    Memory carries great importance among the fey. Their immortality allows them to preserve debts, lineages, victories, losses, and promises long after mortal records have faded. Fey often serve as scribes, archivists, and record keepers. In some communities, they maintain histories extending beyond the institutions that employ them.

    They are patient enough to preserve what others forget and persistent enough to notice when someone would prefer a record to disappear.

    Fey are often secretive among the other peoples of Talvaren. They do not offer trust easily, and even those who live openly may reveal little about Eldevaren or the obligations they carry. Outsiders who earn their confidence may be permitted to hear things that have never been written.

    Vinestrum is the closest thing many outsiders will ever see to a fey city. It stands within ancient verdant woods whose paths shift and disappear. Its dwellings are formed through pact rather than ordinary construction, with enormous trees holding homes and bridges among their branches. At the city’s heart stands the toadstool ring through which the fey may return home—for a while.

    Fey endure because they remember.

    They are marked because promises have weight.

    And once a fey gives their word, they keep it.

    -The Archivist

  • deep delvers

    Subterranean witnesses and archivists searching for freedom.

    Homeland
    The Vast Below
    Age expectation
    Approximately 100 years
    Racial bonus
    See clearly in absolute darkness; take half damage from Vast Below biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Cannot hold their breath; underwater health loss begins immediately

    deep delvers are born of the Vast Below.

    Their origin is unknown. No surviving account identifies the first deep delver, the place from which they emerged, or what shaped them into the people they became. This absence is particularly offensive to a race that has spent generations recording nearly everything else.

    They are pale, slender, and adapted to a world untouched by sunlight. Their eyes perceive absolute darkness as clearly as surface peoples see by day. They live for approximately a century, building families, communities, and ordinary domestic lives within conditions most outsiders consider profoundly hostile.

    Nothing about life in the Vast Below is ordinary.

    Homes must account for stone, depth, isolation, unstable passages, and the vast distances separating one inhabited place from another. Routes may fail. Tunnels may collapse. Entire settlements may depend upon a single passage remaining open. Deep delver culture has adapted to these realities in every respect.

    Meals, courtship, trade, child-rearing, travel, and burial all continue beneath the earth.

    They simply continue with better records.

    deep delvers serve as witnesses, archivists, and intermediaries between the surface and the Vast Below. Routes are logged. Claims are archived. Agreements are witnessed. Discoveries are catalogued, and precedent is preserved so that old disasters do not need to be repeated merely because everyone involved has died.

    They are not loved.

    They are relied upon.

    Their devotion to order did not begin as a virtue.

    During an unknown period of history, the deep delvers entered into an agreement with Vallinoraxistup. They promised him a great service: the location of a surviving female Dragon.

    He paid in advance with a True Unique.

    The deep delvers accepted the payment despite not possessing the information he sought. They believed they could cheat him, keep what he had given them, and escape the consequences of an unfulfilled promise.

    They were mistaken.

    Vallinoraxistup subjugated their entire race.

    The deep delvers are bound to serve him until the surviving female Dragon is found. Generations have inherited a sentence created by an ancient act of dishonesty, and no passage of time has weakened its terms.

    Their records are the machinery of that sentence.

    They document Talvaren because the smallest surviving account may contain what Vallinoraxistup seeks. A forgotten traveler, an unusual excavation, a witness dismissed as foolish, or an object recovered without explanation might provide the thread that ends their obligation.

    Nothing may be discarded because no one knows which detail will free them.

    Their organization is therefore not limited to scribes and archives. Their entire culture has been shaped by the search. Merchants track the movement of rare goods. Travelers record altered routes. Antiquities dealers identify recovered objects and preserve their known histories. Witnesses document claims, discoveries, disappearances, and rumors that other peoples would allow to fade.

    Even failure is recorded.

    A failed search prevents another generation from wasting time in the same place.

    The deep delvers and the fey are firm allies.

    Both peoples understand that words may outlive those who speak them and that an obligation does not become less real merely because it has become inconvenient. Where others see obsessive record-keeping, the fey recognize the weight of a promise carried across generations.

    At the lower terminus of the Great Lift stands a deep delver city whose name has not yet passed into surface use.

    The Great Lift connects that city to Sabenhal and serves as a caravan route between the surface and the Vast Below. It consists of two immense platforms operating side by side. As one rises, the other descends. Each journey lasts fifty-five minutes, followed by five minutes of rest before the platforms depart again.

    Travelers who remain with the lift may rely upon its measured rhythm.

    Those who abandon it for the surrounding tunnels and caves must accept that it will not wait for them.

    deep delvers maintain markets throughout the Vast Below and participate heavily in the movement of recovered objects, historical records, and subterranean goods. Surface merchants depend upon their knowledge even when they distrust the people providing it. Adventurers depend upon their maps even when they ignore every warning written upon them.

    When those adventurers fail to return, the deep delvers record that too.

    Their authority persists because Talvaren remembers what happened the last time their obligations escalated beyond negotiation. They rarely need affection, admiration, or gratitude. A witnessed agreement, an accurate record, and a route that remains where the map claims it should be are usually sufficient.

    deep delvers endure because they document.

    They document because somewhere in the history of Talvaren is the answer that will free them.

    Until she is found, nothing may be forgotten.

    -The Archivist

  • stitchlings

    Created fabric constructs who awakened as people.

    Homeland
    Cindevale
    Age expectation
    Do not age
    Racial bonus
    Take half damage from Stormlands biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Not yet established

    stitchlings were made rather than born.

    The oldest surviving records place their emergence during the Age of Accord, when magic was increasingly used to create, alter, and awaken forms of life. The records disagree about who first made them, why they were made, and whether their awakening was intentional. What is not disputed is that the constructs became people. They developed individual minds, desires, loyalties, ambitions, and flaws—and eventually became an ordinary part of Talvaren’s societies.

    A stitchling may be made from nearly any fabric or flexible material: cotton, linen, burlap, velvet, silk, denim, canvas, sailcloth, leather, or combinations of several materials. Their bodies are not merely clothing. The quality, rarity, color, weave, and condition of their materials are visible parts of their identity.

    stitchlings are vain.

    Silk, velvet, brocade, and rare imported fabrics are associated with wealth, refinement, and social importance. Leather is often associated with warriors, hunters, guards, and adventurers. Denim, canvas, burlap, and other sturdy work fabrics are commonly linked with laborers, builders, farmers, and practical trades. Linen may suggest scholarship, healing, or tradition. Sailcloth may bring to mind sailors, explorers, and coastal communities.

    These associations are expectations, not laws. A silk stitchling may work at a dock. A leather stitchling may become a poet. A denim stitchling may rise into diplomacy and make every fashionable person in the room quietly furious.

    stitchlings can change their fabric over time. Doing so is commonly understood as raising one’s status. A new panel of fine cloth may represent wealth, achievement, marriage, independence, or social ambition. Families may save for years to give a child silk for a birthday, coming-of-age ceremony, or major accomplishment. Such gifts are not casual presents. They are declarations of love, pride, and faith in the stitchling’s future.

    Many stitchlings maintain several appearances. Formal fabric may be reserved for ceremonies and important meetings. Durable cloth may be worn for work. Travel attire may be repaired, weatherproofed, and deliberately plain. Some keep extravagant fabrics for festivals, courtship, or social competition. Changing one’s appearance can be fashion, self-expression, practicality, or calculated social maneuvering.

    stitchlings have no “official” governing hierarchy shared by the entire people. Their communities organize themselves according to local custom, occupation, wealth, age, or reputation. Even without formal ranks, informal status is constantly observed and judged through fabric quality, tailoring, dye, stitching, repairs, provenance, and presentation.

    Their vanity does not mean they reject imperfection. A repair may be embarrassing, beautiful, or deeply meaningful depending on who sees it. A visible patch can mark poverty, injury, survival, sentiment, or stubborn pride. Altering one’s own stitching is personal expression. Altering another stitchling without permission is an intimate violation—closer to harming the body than changing clothing.

    stitchlings are accepted as citizens throughout much of Talvaren. They form families, pursue professions, join guilds, practice faiths, own property, and participate in ordinary society. Their unusual origins do not make their personhood unusual.

    stitchlings often find unexpected kinship with the undead. Both peoples understand what it means to be judged by appearance, questioned as “natural” people, or treated as objects before being recognized as individuals. stitchlings are rarely disturbed by corpses, crypts, decay, or undead appearance. They are also incapable of smelling, which makes them unusually comfortable around the undead—and occasionally unaware that their own wet leather, mildew, smoke, or storage-trunk aroma has become everyone else’s problem.

    They endure because they can be repaired.

    They rise because they can be remade.

    And they remain themselves through every change.

    -The Archivist

  • undead

    People whose lives did not end when death arrived.

    Homeland
    Mirekel
    Age expectation
    Do not age
    Racial bonus
    Do not require air underwater; take half damage from Marsh biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Not yet established

    undead are people whose lives did not end when death arrived.

    They do not bleed, breathe, age, or heal as the living do. Some bear obvious signs of death. Others are remarkably well preserved. Their condition may shape the care their bodies require, but it does not determine their personality, profession, morality, or place in society.

    Undeath became widespread during the Age of the Living Dead, after the destruction of the arachni damaged death itself. Some returned unwillingly. Others were preserved through accident, ritual, disease, or unfinished magic. Entire communities found themselves caught between life and death before anyone understood what had happened.

    The living responded first with fear.

    Holy purges destroyed countless undead because frightened people refused to distinguish dangerous creatures from harmless citizens. Over time, undeath stabilized and understanding replaced panic. Care replaced extermination. Laws were written, institutions adapted, and the undead became an accepted part of Talvaren.

    They work, love, argue, own property, pursue professions, form families, and participate in ordinary society. They are not monsters or symbols by default. They are people whose bodies function differently from those of the living.

    Necromancy developed as a practical response to their needs. Necromancers preserve damaged bodies, restore function, prevent degradation, and correct failures caused by altered death. The practice is openly regulated, and undead seeking treatment are regarded as patients rather than curiosities.

    Mirekel is the home of the undead.

    The city lies among deep marshlands, sprawling bogs, and verdant forests. Undead dwellings extend throughout the surrounding wetlands, while an underwater kingdom is believed to exist beneath the nearby waters. Undead travel to Mirekel from across Talvaren for care, treatment, and the hope offered by its academy, which continues searching for a permanent cure.

    Mirekel is remarkably pristine.

    Its homes, streets, workshops, and public spaces are maintained with exceptional care. The undead of Mirekel take great pride in their clean city and tend to regard disorder as a personal insult. Mud may surround the city, but it is not permitted to win.

    Not every undead person desires a cure. Some consider undeath an affliction. Others have lived with it for so long that removing it would feel like becoming someone else. Mirekel allows those arguments to continue while caring for the people having them.

    Dangerous undead still exist. Unstable alterations, unnatural bindings, and creatures stripped of reason threaten both the living and the dead. Their existence does not make every undead citizen dangerous, any more than the existence of a living murderer condemns every living person.

    undead often find unexpected kinship with stitchlings.

    Both peoples understand what it means to be judged by appearance, questioned as “natural” people, or treated as objects before being recognized as individuals. stitchlings are rarely disturbed by corpses, crypts, decay, or the visible consequences of undeath.

    The undead, in turn, appreciate having friends who are good with a needle and thread.

    After all, stitch folk are not the only people who occasionally appreciate a quick suture.

    undead are those who continued.

    They endure because death did not end them.

    They belong because they are still people.

    And Talvaren has learned not to argue with the evidence.

    -The Archivist

  • therians

    A canine people shaped by the desert and communal life.

    Homeland
    Corinvale
    Age expectation
    80 years
    Racial bonus
    Take half damage from Desert biome hazards
    Racial penalty
    Not yet established

    therians are a canine people shaped by the desert.

    Most therian communities are nomadic. Their caravans are homes, workplaces, marketplaces, and gathering places carried from one region to another. Movement is not considered a sign of poverty or instability. It is simply the way many therians understand life.

    Some therians settle in towns and cities. They may build permanent homes, establish businesses, and remain in one place for generations. Even so, settled therians often maintain strong ties to traveling relatives, seasonal gatherings, and the communities from which their families came.

    therian communities are not one uniform culture. Caravans, families, settlements, and traveling communities develop their own customs, reputations, rivalries, and traditions. Some are known for trade. Others are respected for craft, agriculture, scholarship, healing, or defense. A therian may be judged by the community that raised them as much as by their family or profession.

    therian communities are communal without being peaceful.

    They share food, work, responsibility, and protection, but they also argue, gossip, compete, boast, and hold grudges. Old disagreements may survive for generations. So may old friendships. Gatherings between distant communities are opportunities for trade, celebration, marriage, judgment, remembrance, and settling disputes that have traveled farther than the people involved.

    therians do not make friends easily.

    They are cautious with outsiders and slow to offer trust. A therian may listen carefully while appearing distant, measuring a person by their actions rather than their promises. Friendship is not given casually, and loyalty is not expected without reason.

    Once a therian decides that someone is worthy, however, their loyalty is fierce. Friends, family, and accepted members of the community are defended with remarkable determination. A therian may forgive a great deal from someone they trust. Betrayal is another matter.

    therians have a general distrust of humans.

    The two peoples have been allies and enemies at different points in history. Neither side has forgotten the times the other offered help, broke faith, shared danger, or caused harm. Some therians believe humans are too quick to make promises and too willing to change agreements when doing so benefits them.

    This distrust is not universal hatred. Individual humans are judged by their actions. A human who proves dependable may earn a therian’s respect slowly—and keep it for life.

    therians are proud of their appearance, their communities, and their ability to endure difficult conditions. They are also subject to the ordinary indignities of life. Shedding season is particularly disruptive. Grooming, brushing, cleaning, and removing fur from clothing, bedding, food, and furniture become unavoidable responsibilities.

    In many therian communities, unnecessary work stops until the shedding is under control.

    This is considered practical.

    It is also considered an excellent excuse.

    therians endure because they know how to live together.

    They survive because they know whom to trust.

    And once they call someone their own, they do not abandon them easily.

    -The Archivist

  • arachni

    The arachni enter surviving history at the close of the Age of Prosperity, arriving not as settlers, but as conquerors. They broke kingdoms, occupied cities, and enslaved those they did not destroy.

    They are spider beings of exceptional intelligence, governed by a matriarchy of three queens. The eldest among them are said to assume humanoid form, though records disagree on whether this is transformation, illusion, or simply another reason never to trust a stranger with too many opinions about webbing.

    Twice during the Age of Subjugation, attempts were made to broker peace with the arachni.

    Both failed.

    The surviving accounts provide no reason to believe a third attempt would produce a more imaginative result.

    The arachni hunt all peoples not of their own race. Captives are fed upon gradually until thought, memory, and identity have been stripped away. When nothing remains but obedient flesh, the victim becomes what historians call a revenant: a living corpse used for menial labor or sent ahead as disposable combat fodder. Once this condition is reached, no recovery has ever been recorded.

    Some scholars believe this feeding produced the first undead. Others argue that death itself was damaged when Vallinoraxistup nearly eradicated the arachni and ended their empire. The distinction is apparently important to theologians. It has proven considerably less useful to the victims.

    Reports from recent generations suggest the arachni are rebuilding their numbers, possibly in preparation for another campaign. Curiously, no reliable sighting has placed them anywhere within the Vast Below in living memory.

    One final matter has been demanded for inclusion.

    Unsubstantiated reports claim that arachni avoid kobolds. Kobolds insist this is because arachni recognize their alleged dragon blood.

    They do not possess dragon blood.

    I have already recorded this.

    Apparently, once was insufficient.

    -The Archivist

  • shades

    Why the hell I have been instructed to include these bastards in a history of Talvaren’s peoples, I cannot say.

    They have founded no kingdom, signed no treaty, and preserved no history. Nevertheless, I have been ordered to record what is known.

    Shades began appearing noticeably after the Fey Accords. No account describes their arrival. They were simply absent—and then they were not. The fey may know more, but questioning them has produced answers that are technically complete, perfectly truthful, and utterly useless.

    Shades are translucent figures, commonly gray or blue in appearance, who hover just above the ground. They leave no tracks and cannot be struck, restrained, obstructed, or persuaded to go elsewhere.

    Their forms, clothing, and possessions appear drawn from innumerable cultures. Some are familiar. Others belong to no recorded people or period in Talvaren’s history. Shades have appeared as musicians, warriors, nobles, barbarians, and soldiers carrying weapons no living smith could construct. Several possess devices they call “mobile phones.” One recently attempted to purchase festival cakes using a “credit card.”

    The merchant’s refusal resulted in an argument lasting three hours.

    Shades cannot affect Talvaren physically. Their weapons cause no wounds, their hands move no objects, and their possessions cannot be taken or examined. They can, however, interact with one another. They marry, quarrel, perform music, stage battles, and gather in considerable numbers during festivals and holidays.

    It has now demanded that I formally acknowledge the existence of kobold shades.

    There. Acknowledged.

    I have recorded this incident so that, should I lose my damned mind before completing this assignment, the archives will contain documented proof that the decline was provoked.

  • kobolds

    By official instruction, kobolds are to be included among the recognized peoples of Talvaren.

    I objected.

    My objection was returned with the word “pests” crossed out and “people” written above it.

    Very well.

    Kobolds originated in the Vast Below, where they occupied secondary tunnels, abandoned passages, and whatever spaces larger civilizations failed to watch carefully enough. Kobolds insist these places were freely offered to them.

    They were not.

    At some unknown point, most kobolds were driven toward the surface. No surviving account identifies what displaced them, and whatever caused the migration does not appear to have followed. Kobolds have supplied numerous explanations, including a territorial dispute with an underground moon, the collapse of an ancient dragon empire, and “too many spiders.”

    None has been substantiated.

    Modern kobolds are small, scaled humanoids with large pointed ears, tails, clawed hands and feet, and considerable variation in their snouts, crests, spines, and facial structure. They are social, persistent, rarely violent, and capable of adapting to nearly any settlement willing—or insufficiently prepared—to accommodate them.

    They have developed a particular fondness for open skies, readily available food, and what they collectively call “shineys.” This category includes coins, tools, jewelry, household objects, personal belongings, unattended equipment, and nearly anything else that catches a kobold’s interest.

    I initially attempted to produce a complete definition of the term.

    Three pages later, one of my reference books was classified as a shiney and disappeared.

    Understanding kobold society requires accepting that kobolds do not recognize trespassing or theft as other peoples define them.

    An unlocked entrance is permission to enter.

    For the sake of clarity, kobolds consider an entrance unlocked if the door is open, partially open, closed but unlatched, latched but accessible, secured by an easily picked lock, or protected by any mechanism that can be overcome with sufficient patience. An accessible window also constitutes an entrance.

    In practical terms, a door is locked only when it successfully prevents a kobold from opening it.

    This distinction has not been helpful in court.

    Once inside, kobolds sincerely believe themselves welcome. The absence of an invitation is regarded as an unfortunate oversight by the host rather than evidence that entry was forbidden. An attempt to remove them may be interpreted as poor hospitality, an unfamiliar local custom, or the beginning of a game.

    None of these interpretations improves the situation.

    Kobolds apply similar reasoning to possessions. If an item was not watched closely enough to prevent it from wandering away, its owner clearly did not want it. A kobold would never be so discourteous as to leave another person’s belongings unappreciated.

    Yes, they describe the belongings as having wandered.

    No, pointing out that the belongings were carried away inside a kobold’s pocket does not alter this conclusion.

    This belief is sincere and entirely reciprocal. Kobolds express little anger when their own possessions disappear. They generally assume another person admired the object, recognized that it was insufficiently guarded, and accepted responsibility for appreciating it properly.

    They consider this generous.

    Everyone else considers it theft.

    Apparently, both positions are to be represented in the historical record.

    Kobolds are master pickpockets. Their small hands, patience, confidence, and complete absence of guilt make them extraordinarily skilled at removing possessions without notice. When discovered, they rarely understand why the former owner is upset. From the kobold’s perspective, the item was neglected, rescued, and given a more attentive home.

    Some settlements have established designated kobold quarters and negotiated rules governing entry into homes, the borrowing of possessions, and the return of particularly important shineys. These agreements function until a kobold discovers an unlocked window, an unattended key, or an object whose owner appeared insufficiently enthusiastic about possessing it.

    Other settlements regard kobolds as vermin or criminals and kill them on sight.

    Kobolds find this reaction excessive.

    They do not consider themselves invaders. They believe they are welcome, helpful, and remarkably generous guests. The fact that nobody invited them is dismissed as a minor failure of communication on the host’s part.

    They also insist that they possess dragon blood.

    They do not.

    Kobolds cite their scales, tails, underground ancestry, personal confidence, and an unsubstantiated claim that arachni avoid them as proof of draconic descent. None of these constitutes evidence. This was explained in the arachni record. Repeating the claim here has not improved it.

    During the preparation of this entry, two quills, an inkstone, one shoe, and my exceptionally expensive brass magnifying glass disappeared.

    When informed of the missing items, one of the kobolds immediately volunteered to help.

    It produced my magnifying glass and began examining the room through it.

    I explained that the magnifying glass it was using was among the items we were attempting to find.

    The kobold looked at the glass. It looked at me. It then announced, with considerable satisfaction, that we could remove the magnifying glass from the list because it had found it.

    I have been instructed to describe this behavior as helpful.

    Kobolds are neither monsters nor innocents. They are small, sociable, extraordinarily capable intruders who will enter your home, empty your pockets, compliment your hospitality, and feel genuinely wounded that you failed to take anything of theirs.

    And they are, by official decree, an acknowledged people of Talvaren.

    I have recorded that statement exactly as instructed.

    Lock your doors.

    Properly.

    No, a latch does not count.

    Apparently neither does a lock.

    I am no longer offering practical advice.

    -The Archivist (whose patience was apparently left unattended)
