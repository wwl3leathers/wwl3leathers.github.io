History

Public record

Talvaren’s history is not a complete account. The oldest records were written long after the events they describe. Some were carved into stone, some preserved by institutions, and some survived only as fragments repeated by people who no longer understood their origins.

The world does not agree on everything that happened. It agrees that the Ages were real. They are not remembered as a simple march toward progress. Each Age solved certain problems while creating others. Civilizations endured, adapted, and occasionally mistook survival for wisdom.

The Age of Dragonkin

The earliest surviving records describe a world saturated with mana.

dragonkin civilizations dominated the surface, not because they were united, but because magic had become so abundant that ordinary limits no longer applied. Dragons built cities that could defend themselves, roads that remembered travelers, and monuments whose purposes have not yet been understood.

Magic was treated as a resource without an ending. That assumption failed when a resurrection ritual was attempted on a scale later considered unforgivable. The ritual did not restore what had been lost. It created the Nullbrand Revenant—a being whose existence threatened to turn death, memory, and magic against the living world.

The dragonkin responded with sacrifice. Most Dragons perished or withdrew from the world to create a binding strong enough to contain the result. The prison held, but the civilization that created it did not survive unchanged.

The Age ended not with conquest or fire, but with retreat. Mana diminished. dragonkin influence faded. Their cities became ruins, their roads became legends, and their remaining descendants disappeared into places the surface no longer knew how to reach.

The Age of Prosperity

With the dragonkin gone from daily life, mortal civilizations expanded into the space they left behind.

Kingdoms formed along rivers, coastlines, and mountain passes. Trade routes became permanent. Settlements grew large enough to require laws, records, and people whose entire purpose was to maintain what others built.

human kingdoms were not alone. therian communities traveled the trade roads, crossed the deserts, and carried knowledge between settlements that would otherwise have remained strangers. They built fewer monuments than the dwarves and claimed less territory than the kingdoms around them, but their routes connected the growing world.

Among the peoples of the Age, the dwarves became the great builders. They raised kingdoms beneath mountains and across high valleys. They traded with every known people, carrying metalwork, stonecraft, tools, weapons, and engineering across the world. Their roads connected distant cultures long before most nations understood the value of connection.

Dwarven monuments still survive. Some remain visible as towers, bridges, gates, and buried halls. Others are hidden beneath later settlements or mistaken for natural formations. Their builders are gone, but their work continues to shape the world.

This was an age of confidence. Mortals believed that the world could be made understandable if enough roads were mapped, enough ruins opened, and enough old powers were studied.

That confidence ended when the arachni emerged. As arachni dominance began, the dwarven kingdoms disappeared from the surface. Some records describe war. Others describe retreat, burial, or deliberate withdrawal. No surviving account agrees on what happened.

The monuments remained. The dwarves did not.

The Age of Subjugation

The arachni did not arrive as teachers, allies, or negotiators. They arrived as conquerors.

They believed themselves to be supreme beings, and they treated every other people as material to be conquered, organized, and used. Kingdoms were broken. Cities were occupied. Laws were rewritten to serve arachni authority.

Those who resisted were destroyed. Those who surrendered were enslaved. The arachni did not integrate into the world. They attempted to replace it.

Their rule lasted long enough to become ordinary. Generations were born beneath arachni dominion and learned to mistake survival for acceptance. Existing institutions were preserved only when they proved useful. Traditions were tolerated only when they no longer threatened obedience.

The arachni believed that nothing in creation stood above them. They were wrong.

Vallinoraxistup was one of the last known Dragons. The arachni attempted to destroy him, apparently believing that even an ancient Dragon could be treated as another obstacle to remove. They did not understand what they had challenged.

What followed was not a war. It was not even a battle in the traditional sense. Vallinoraxistup eradicated nearly the entire arachni race almost single-handedly, and with such finality that later generations struggled to understand how quickly their empire disappeared.

He was not acting as a savior. He was not defending the innocent. He had been insulted. That was enough.

The response left a permanent mark upon Talvaren. A region of the world was scarred by the event, its ruins and altered landscape becoming evidence of a power that had not needed an army, a campaign, or permission.

The arachni had ruled for generations. Vallinoraxistup ended them almost incidentally. The Age of Subjugation ended in a silence deeper than victory.

The Age of the Living Dead

The destruction of the arachni damaged more than civilization. It damaged death.

Undeath spread faster than existing institutions could understand. Some people returned unwillingly. Others were preserved by accident, ritual, disease, or unfinished magic. Entire communities were caught between life and death while the living world responded with fear.

Holy purges followed. Large populations were destroyed—not because every undead person was dangerous, but because fear made distinction feel irresponsible.

Eventually, the world learned that death had not been broken completely. It had been altered.

Undead populations stabilized. Communities formed. Necromancy emerged not as a practice of domination, but as a medical and restorative discipline. Care replaced panic. Law replaced extermination.

The undead became citizens. Not everyone accepted this willingly. Some never did. But institutions gradually learned to account for people who did not bleed, breathe, age, or heal in the same way.

The Age ended when the world stopped asking whether the undead had the right to remain and began asking how society would remain whole with them in it.

The Age of Accord

The next Age began with negotiation.

Mortals entered into lawful agreements with the fey. Superstition was not replaced by trust, exactly, but by terms, witnesses, and consequences that could be recorded.

Charters replaced panic. Promises became enforceable across generations.

The fey did not make the world safer. They made it more legible. A bargain could still be dangerous, but danger could now be named, witnessed, and remembered.

Guilds expanded rapidly during this period. Registries, professions, civic offices, and formal routes developed alongside new magical infrastructure. Knowledge was archived rather than hoarded.

By this era, the deep delvers had become established record-keepers and intermediaries between the surface and the Vast Below. Routes were logged. Claims were preserved. Old escalations became precedents instead of repeated disasters.

The stitchlings appeared during this period. No surviving record identifies the first awakening. Some scholars believe the increased ambient magic surrounding the fey accords allowed prepared cloth and crafted forms to develop consciousness. Others argue that stitchlings had existed earlier and simply lacked the institutions necessary to be recognized.

Whatever the truth, stitchlings became part of the world. Linen, leather, denim, silk, sailcloth, and burlap learned to move, speak, repair themselves, and form identities separate from their makers. They were created, but creation did not make them property.

By the end of the Age, Talvaren had become administratively legible. Not safe. Not simple. Legible enough to build upon.

The Age of Quiet Balance

The current Age is defined by stability that no one entirely trusts.

Empires rise and fall within understood limits. Ancient powers remain dormant. The roads continue to function, provided someone maintains them. True Uniques circulate rarely. Institutions endure because enough people remember what happened the last time they failed.

Most citizens experience the world as manageable. That word matters.

Talvaren is not peaceful. Danger remains in the wilderness, beneath the mountains, across the sea, and inside systems that have not yet failed loudly enough to be noticed.

The world is also entering a new kind of era. Steam-powered devices are beginning to appear alongside magitech systems. Engines, pressure vessels, enchanted mechanisms, and practical arcane tools are slowly changing how people travel, build, communicate, and work. No one yet knows whether this development will improve the world or merely give old ambitions better instruments.

Almost as an afterthought, kobolds began appearing in settled regions. They are generally regarded as pests of a particularly persistent sort, but most communities tolerate them when containment, trade, or exhaustion makes extermination less practical.

Shades also began appearing. They leave no records, answer no useful questions, and seem to have arrived without an event anyone can identify. They watch, comment, and remain.

The Age of Quiet Balance is regarded as an achievement, though not a permanent one. Its stability rests on accumulated knowledge, maintained infrastructure, negotiated obligation, and the continued cooperation of powers that have failed before.

The world does not claim to be finished. It only claims to be continuing.

A record is not the same thing as the truth.

The public history of Talvaren preserves what its peoples agree is worth remembering. Older records remain incomplete, disputed, and occasionally dangerous to interpret.
